# Western Hognose Snake
**Scientific name:** Heterodon nasicus
**Difficulty:** Intermediate

## Overview
Western hognose snakes are small fossorial snakes from the western United States, with parts of their range extending into southern Canada and northern Mexico. They favor shortgrass prairie, dry rocky areas, and sandy soils. Adults may reach up to about 35 inches, with males usually much smaller than females.

This species has an upturned snout, keeled scales, a blunt triangular head, and many captive color morphs. Western hognoses are popular for their size and behavior, but they can be picky feeders. They are rear-fanged venomous; bites are not usually medically significant for humans but can cause swelling and discomfort. Captives commonly live 10-15 years.

## Enclosure
Use a front-opening enclosure no smaller than 36 x 18 x 18 inches for one adult, with larger housing preferred for large females. Floor space and substrate depth are especially important because this species burrows. House singly; snakes do not benefit from cohabitation and may compete for food, heat, and security.

## Lighting and UVB
Western hognose snakes are diurnal and benefit from a clear day-night cycle. Provide about 12 hours of light daily. UVB is recommended at modest levels: use a forest-strength linear lamp over the warm side, ideally spanning about half the enclosure. Aim for a basking UVI around 2.0-3.0. Depending on fixture and mesh, the basking area may sit roughly 6-15 inches below the lamp. A separate 6000-7000K daylight fixture can improve brightness and activity.

## Heating
Use overhead halogen or white heat bulbs to create a broad basking area. Target a basking surface of 90-95°F and a cool side of 70-75°F. Multiple low to moderate wattage bulbs can warm the snake's coiled body more evenly than a single tight hot spot. Turn heat lamps off at night unless the room drops below safe temperatures. Measure surfaces with an infrared thermometer and monitor hides with digital probes.

## Humidity
Maintain average humidity around 30-50%, measured near the middle of the enclosure. It is normal for the cool side and nighttime readings to be higher. Mist lightly as needed, and use leaf litter or a moisture-retentive substrate to help stabilize humidity. Avoid persistently wet conditions.

## Substrate
Provide at least 4 inches of burrowable substrate; deeper is better. Soil-like reptile substrates, hemp, shredded aspen, or a DIY mix of about 80% organic additive-free topsoil and 20% play sand can work. Add clean leaf litter for cover and enrichment. Spot-clean waste promptly and replace the substrate every 3-4 months or as needed.

## Decor and Enrichment
Provide multiple hides, cork logs, hollow pieces, sturdy branches, plants, and clutter across the enclosure. The snake should be able to travel from warm to cool areas while staying partly concealed. Include a stable basking stone or paver under the lamps.

## Diet
Western hognose snakes are carnivores. Hatchlings may eat a pinky mouse every 3-4 days. Adults may take appropriately sized prey every 4-7 days, depending on body condition, age, and meal size. Prey should be about the same diameter as the snake's head or only slightly larger. Increase prey size gradually as the snake grows.

Although mice are common, variety is useful because wild hognoses often specialize in amphibians. Options include young mice, young rats, quail eggs, captive-bred frogs or toads, green anoles where legal and appropriate, and reptile sausage-style whole-prey products. Frozen-thawed prey is safer than live prey. Thaw in a sealed bag in warm water and offer with long feeding tongs. If the snake refuses, amphibian or lizard scenting may help.

## Supplements
Whole vertebrate prey usually provides balanced nutrition without routine supplementation. Supplements are generally unnecessary unless directed by a veterinarian or when using incomplete prey items.

## Water
Keep clean water available at all times in a bowl large enough for the snake to curl into if it chooses. Replace daily or when soiled.

## Safety Notes
Use feeding tongs to reduce accidental bites. Handle calmly and avoid smelling like prey. Because this species is rear-fanged venomous, people with allergies, immune concerns, or a history of strong reactions should be especially cautious. Seek medical advice for severe swelling, spreading pain, or systemic symptoms after a bite.
