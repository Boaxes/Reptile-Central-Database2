# Ball Python
**Scientific name:** Python regius
**Difficulty:** Easy - Intermediate

## Overview
Ball pythons are medium-bodied constrictors from western and central Africa. They are usually 3-5 feet long, with females often larger than males. Wild-type animals are brown and black with pale outlines and a light belly, while captive breeding has produced many color and pattern morphs. They are generally slow-moving, secretive, and tolerant of gentle handling. With proper care, ball pythons can live 30 years or more.

## Enclosure
Use a secure enclosure no smaller than 48 inches long, 24 inches wide, and 24 inches tall for an adult. Front-opening enclosures are easiest to maintain. Provide at least two snug hides, one warm and one cool, plus enough cover for the snake to move without feeling exposed. House ball pythons singly; sharing space causes competition for heat, hides, food, and security.

## Lighting and UVB
Ball pythons are crepuscular, but they still benefit from a consistent day/night cycle and low to moderate UVB. Use a 22 inch T5 HO forest-style UVB lamp on the warm side. Aim for a basking UVI around 2.0-3.0. Approximate placement is 9-11 inches below a lamp over mesh or 15-18 inches below an unobstructed internal lamp. Provide a regular daytime light cycle, usually about 12 hours on and 12 hours off.

## Heating
Create a warm basking zone and a cooler retreat. Target a basking surface of 95-104°F, a warm hide of 86-90°F, a cool hide of 72-80°F, and nighttime temperatures around 72-78°F. Halogen flood lamps over a basking stone or hide work well for daytime heat. If the warm hide does not reach target temperatures, a thermostat-controlled heat mat under the hide can be used, covered by substrate so the snake cannot contact it directly. Use an infrared thermometer for surfaces and digital probes for hide temperatures.

## Humidity
Maintain a humidity range of about 45-75%, with the cool hide being the most humid area. Humidity that stays too low can cause dehydration and poor sheds, while constantly wet conditions can contribute to illness. Mist in the morning and evening as needed, add damp sphagnum moss to the cool hide, and use leaf litter to stabilize moisture.

## Substrate
Use 2-4 inches of moisture-retentive, soil-like substrate. Reptile soil, coconut fiber, cypress-style mulch, or a mix of organic topsoil, reptile soil, and play sand can all work. Deeper substrate helps buffer humidity even though ball pythons are not heavy diggers. Spot-clean waste promptly and replace all substrate about every 3-4 months.

## Decor and Enrichment
Provide more than empty hides. Ball pythons climb, explore, and use cover when given the chance. Add cork logs, sturdy branches, artificial or live plants, leaf litter, and visual barriers. Branches should be secure and sized to support the snake. Keep pathways open enough for movement but cluttered enough that the snake can feel hidden.

## Diet
Ball pythons are carnivores that eat whole prey. Feed hatchlings about every 5 days, small juveniles every 7 days, larger juveniles every 7-14 days, subadults and moderate adults every 14-21 days, and large adults every 28-56 days depending on body condition. A typical meal should total about 10% of the snake's body weight and should not exceed 1.5 times the snake's widest point. Offer variety when possible, including mice, rats, African soft-furred rats, quail, or chicks. Frozen-thawed prey is safer than live prey and should be warmed to about body temperature before feeding.

## Supplements
Whole prey is usually nutritionally complete, but occasional supplementation can help prevent gaps in limited diets. Lightly dust prey now and then with a small amount of calcium and multivitamin, or use a reptile supplement according to label directions.

## Water
Provide a large, stable bowl of fresh water at all times. The bowl should be big enough for soaking. Replace water daily or whenever soiled, and disinfect the bowl regularly.

## Safety Notes
Use feeding tongs to reduce accidental bites. Secure the enclosure carefully, as snakes are skilled escape artists. Avoid live prey unless under expert supervision, because rodents can injure or kill a snake.
